## Corrections and limitations

- progressive-traditionalist, question 6: Despite expanded women’s participation and social reforms, the ordinary family model in this scoped record remained a married parental household. Qualified preference for that model is more likely than treating recognition of alternative families as complete neutrality about a preferred model.
- progressive-traditionalist, question 9: The scoped institutions and conventional family/sexuality norms did not provide equal social recognition to same-sex adoptive parents. A qualified rejection is the most likely historical analogy; absence of an exact statement is not a reason to automatically choose Neutral.
- progressive-traditionalist, question 10: Conventional public modesty remained compatible with this period’s social programme. A qualified preference for modest dress is more likely than claiming the reforms erased that expectation; this does not imply a legal clothing mandate.
- progressive-traditionalist, question 12: This reform programme challenged inherited hierarchy and sought to transmit changed civic and social commitments. Prioritising established customs over new ones would be a broader conservative rule than the record supports, even though several family traditions persisted.

For every subject, public commitments, coalition implementation and modern analogies were distinguished. Sources were checked against at least three publisher organisations, although Wikipedia synthesises some of the same underlying material and is not independent confirmation of every claim. Some accessible search excerpts and official biographical pages are only contextual, and a third publisher is not independent verification of all answers. Every Culture vs Nature comparison and modern enhancement, AI or admissions mechanism should receive particular human scrutiny. A social reform record is contextual evidence, not a biological doctrine.
