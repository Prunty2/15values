## Scope and evidence boundary

The social liberal tradition of T. H. Green and L. T. Hobhouse, centred on enabling freedom through public provision and social reform within a market economy. Revision2 is an incomplete evidence audit of this specified school. Unsupported modern applications and empirical causal comparisons are null/unknown. Neither agreement nor neutrality is inferred from silence. Historical or programme sources are not a complete contemporary incumbent-government record.

Hobhouse’s Social Evolution and Political Theory, chapters II–III, directly distinguishes social transmission from biological inheritance. The restored Culture answers rely on his concrete comparisons of cooperation, discipline, moral convention and violent conduct. His qualified support for negative eugenics is counter-evidence to any blanket claim that he denies heredity. National IQ, stress, attention, temperament and physical attraction remain unresolved; social progress is not a quantitative explanation of every individual trait.

The draft contains all 240 current question IDs: 92 supported numeric assessments and 148 unresolved entries. Unknown is not a score. No target placement or neighbouring profile was consulted.

## Primary research added in this continuation

- `culture-nature-01`: +1 (inferred); Hobhouse distinguishes relatively stable inherited capacities from the institutions that transform their expression. Chapters II–III assign the predominant role in social behaviour to changing traditions, while retaining biological constraints. Sources: `social-evolution`.
- `culture-nature-05`: +1 (inferred); Chapters II–III explain how institutions cultivate different capacities and make different pursuits available, directing the same underlying qualities toward war, commerce or cooperation. This supports experience and opportunity as the stronger explanation of developed interests. Sources: `social-evolution`.
- `culture-nature-07`: +2 (inferred); Chapter II contrasts panic with socially organised cooperation, explaining improvement through learned rules and institutions rather than biological replacement. Cooperative habits are chiefly socially developed in this account. Sources: `social-evolution`.
- `culture-nature-10`: -1 (inferred); Chapter II describes traditions transmitted between minds as providing largely unreflective rules for ordinary conduct. This gives learned moral customs priority in the content of immediate judgements, without denying inherited impulses. Sources: `social-evolution`.
- `culture-nature-14`: -1 (inferred); Chapter III argues that the same original motives can produce blood-feud violence or peaceful achievement under different social institutions. That specific comparison weighs against attributing group violence primarily to inherited racial characteristics. Sources: `social-evolution`.
- `culture-nature-15`: +1 (inferred); Chapter III identifies discipline as acquired through training, capable of transforming a person’s social functioning. This supports learned self-control over a racial-inheritance explanation, while avoiding a claim that all temperamental differences are learned. Sources: `social-evolution`.
