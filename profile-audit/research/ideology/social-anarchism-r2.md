## Scope and interpretation

The scope is now contemporary social anarchist communism represented by An Anarchist FAQ, with Kropotkin as foundation. This is disclosed rather than assigning modern policies to the 1911 text.

Contemporary social anarchist communism as articulated in An Anarchist FAQ, with Kropotkin’s mutual-aid and common-property theory as its foundation. Present-day policy answers use the FAQ’s explicit distinction between immediate reforms and the eventual stateless society. Defending an existing public service does not endorse permanent government; abolishing government does not endorse private capitalism. Government is read literally, never relabelled a commune. Unrepresented institutional alternatives remain unknown. No incumbent government is treated as embodying this anti-state doctrine.

## Contemporary acts

No incumbent state can embody this anti-state doctrine. Documented views on existing state policies can nevertheless answer some questions: defending state welfare against private domination is explicitly distinguished from the eventual self-managed alternative.

## Counter-evidence and remaining research limits

Many questions assume choosing between a national and regional state, or state planning and capitalist markets. A stateless alternative cannot be scored as agreement with an unendorsed opposite. The FAQ rescues some welfare and service-provision answers but does not prescribe every judicial power, tariff, currency arrangement or biomedical threshold.

Research looked for doctrinal positions rather than contemporary scientific answers to the Culture vs Nature questions. A source’s moral equality claim is not treated as empirical evidence. No score, similarity target or expected ideological location was used. Neutral answers below are explicit mixed or conditional judgements, never placeholders for unknown evidence.

## Changes supported by the new readings

- `public-private-03`: None → -1. J.5.15 defends public provision against privatisation because replacing public services with private firms increases capitalist power; it still seeks eventual self-management. Comparable public/private outcomes do not therefore establish a presumption for private ownership.
- `public-private-07`: None → -1. J.5.15 rejects privatising public services as a route to freedom and prioritises democratic self-management. Lower contracting costs alone do not outweigh the additional employer hierarchy and weakened collective control identified there.
- `public-private-10`: None → 1. J.5.15 supports defending council housing against privatisation while pressing for tenant control. A substantial public stock is supported as an immediate protection, not as the final anarchist ownership system.
- `public-private-13`: None → -1. The FAQ’s immediate opposition to privatisation applies to service delivery, while its desired replacement is community self-management. Contracting routine services to a private employer does not satisfy that objective.
- `high-redistribution-low-redistribution-03`: None → 1. J.5.15 explicitly defends welfare provision that lets people refuse starvation-level work and argues against cuts. This supports improving the income floor as an immediate reform despite the eventual abolition of taxation and the state.
- `high-redistribution-low-redistribution-08`: None → -2. J.5.15 opposes welfare-to-work compulsion and defends continued material support against the threat of destitution. An expiry that leaves capable recipients in poverty recreates precisely that coercion.
- `high-redistribution-low-redistribution-10`: None → -1. J.5.15 rejects welfare cuts justified by shrinking the state when the result strengthens private domination. Keeping benefits deliberately modest to reduce contributors’ taxes conflicts with that immediate defensive policy.
- `high-redistribution-low-redistribution-12`: None → -1. The FAQ distinguishes eventual abolition of the state from immediate tax-cutting and welfare retrenchment; J.5.15 supports defending provision against the latter. This opposes giving tax cuts blanket priority over improved cash protection.
- `culture-nature-01`: None → 1. J.6.3–4 attributes authoritarian, anxious and cooperative patterns of adult behaviour largely to upbringing and social relationships. The text also recognises natural capacities, so the inference is a social-development emphasis, not an absolute blank slate.
- `culture-nature-04`: None → -1. J.6.4 explains persistent anxiety and fear through punitive childhood relationships and subsequent defensive habits. As the scoped current’s explanatory belief, this supports experience over biology in stress sensitivity, without presenting its psychoanalytic claims as established science.
- `culture-nature-07`: None → 1. J.6.3 describes social behaviour developing through supportive relationships and being distorted by coercive upbringing. This makes surroundings central to whether cooperative capacities flourish, while acknowledging a natural social disposition.
- `culture-nature-08`: None → 0. J.6.3 explicitly combines naturally developing empathy with its suppression or flourishing through parenting and social experience. The account assigns causal roles to both, rather than prescribing inherited differences as the predominant explanation of empathy.
- `culture-nature-10`: None → -1. J.6.3–4 explains internalised moral guilt, obedience and ethical reflection through childhood authority and relationships. This supports a qualified learned account of immediate moral reactions rather than their primarily biological origin.
- `culture-nature-11`: None → 1. J.6.4 traces fear, anxiety and perceived scapegoats to authoritarian upbringing and later propaganda. It therefore gives learning and social experience a central explanatory role in what people perceive as threatening.
- `culture-nature-12`: None → -1. J.6.4 describes submissive, authoritarian and anxious adult dispositions as products of disciplinary upbringing. That is a source-based developmental account of temperament, not an inference from an equality commitment.
